LC-PolScope · settings & image formation

What the PolScope settings do, and how often to change them

The concept page covers what birefringence is and how five frames become a retardance map. This page covers what an operator does with the instrument: how the image actually forms in the light path, what the calibration and the background each correct, and which settings stay fixed for a whole study while others must be redone for every slide. All the numbers come from an exact polarization model (Jones calculus) of the same five-state scheme our rig uses, at its 549 nm wavelength and 0.03-wave swing.

0 · the rule you were taught

“Keep every setting the same between acquisitions.” So why redo the background on every slide?

The rule is right, but it applies to the measurement, not the reference. Retardance (nm) and slow-axis angle (°) are physical quantities. You keep them comparable by keeping fixed everything that defines how they are measured. The calibration and the background are references: they record what the instrument reads when no specimen is there. That reading includes the slide's own glass, coverslip and mountant, so a new slide changes it. Think of the tare button on a balance: you press it again for every new weighing boat precisely so that the weights stay comparable.

Hold constant across a study

  • Wavelength (549 nm filter) and swing (0.03 waves)
  • Objective and condenser aperture
  • Reconstruction: 5-state scheme, method, black level for the camera's pixel format
  • Camera mode: auto gain and auto exposure off, gamma off, 12/16-bit
  • Exposure, where the slides allow it
  • Display (retardance ceiling, colour map) for any figures you compare

Re-measure, because they include the slide

  • Background: every slide, after every calibration, after any exposure change, for each objective
  • Calibration: every session (the liquid crystals drift), after any change to the light path, and on a new slide when its extinction ratio comes out low. The recOrder/waveorder guide puts it as “definitely between imaging sessions, often between different samples.”

Redoing these does not make slides incomparable. Skipping them does. In card 4, a background reused from another slide makes a 10.3 nm fibre read 8.5 nm, 7° off its true angle, and every empty pixel reads 3.6 nm at one shared angle. The mosaic still looks seamless.

2 · the five states

Five illumination states, one pixel

The two liquid crystals shape the light reaching the slide. In the extinction state (State0) it is circularly polarized, so the circular analyzer blocks it completely, unless something on the slide changed it. Each of the four swing states moves one crystal by ±0.03 waves, which makes the light slightly elliptical, with its long axis in one of four directions. A birefringent pixel brightens some swing states more than others, and the pattern across the four tells the reconstruction its angle. How far the pixel rises above extinction overall gives its retardance. Pick a state to see it.

3 · calibration

Calibration: finding extinction for the light path you actually have

“Calibrate Liquid Crystals” searches both crystals for the setting where the least light reaches the camera, on a clear, specimen-free field. That minimum is not a fixed number. It moves when the crystals drift (temperature, warm-up, time) and when the slide adds its own uniform residual retardance. The search places extinction wherever the minimum is for the path in front of it, so a calibration silently absorbs the slide it was run on. The map shows extinction-state brightness over the two crystal settings, with dark meaning less light. Drift the crystals or change the slide, and watch the extinction ratio fall until you calibrate again.

slide on the stage

The drift here is illustrative. How quickly the crystals drift on our rig has not been measured. The extinction ratio is the number to watch: QPSC grades it as good at 100 or above and acceptable from 80. This rig has reached about 280. In the model, a calibrated path reaches 300 and the limit is set by stray light, which on a real microscope also falls as the condenser aperture opens.

4 · background

Background correction: what calibration cannot remove

A calibration sets one extinction point for the whole field. Residual retardance that varies across the field stays behind. Strain in the objective and condenser lenses is the main source, and it is identical in every tile, which is why uncorrected mosaics show seams. Glass, coverslip and mountant add a component that differs from slide to slide. The background is five frames taken on a clear area of the same slide, with the same calibration and the same exposure, defocused 10–20 µm so dust drops out. The reconstruction subtracts it pixel by pixel. Retardance adds like an arrow (magnitude and angle), so the subtraction only cancels when the background describes the same path as the acquisition. Mostly one colour in the orientation view? Uncorrected residual retardance has a single angle, so glass and faint tissue all take that angle's hue. Switch to the orientation view with no background, or with the other slide's background, to see it. QPSC refuses a background from a different calibration and reconstructs uncorrected instead, saying why in the server log. It cannot tell that a background came from another slide, so that part is yours: keep a background profile per slide setup and collect on each slide.

    7 · reading results

    Things that look like settings problems, but are not

    Changes the retardance, but is real

    • Section thickness. Retardance is Δn × thickness, so a 7 µm section of the same collagen reads 40% more than a 5 µm one. Compare slides cut at the same thickness, or compare orientation, which thickness does not change.
    • Fibres tilted out of the plane show less retardance than the same fibres lying flat.

    Changes the brightness, not the retardance

    • Stain absorption, or a dimmed area. Anything that dims all five states equally cancels out of the ratios. That includes heavy eosin and the darker circle where the lamp was left on overnight. Retardance should be unaffected, but those pixels are noisier.
    • Exposure. Same retardance, different noise. Recollect the background if you change it.

    Background or calibration taken in the wrong place

    • Specimen in the background field subtracts tissue from every tile, which shows up as ghost negatives. The Ready box warns when the background's swing states disagree by more than 10%.
    • Dust in focus during the background leaves spots in every tile. Defocus 10–20 µm.
    • Birefringent junk in the calibration field pulls extinction off; the extinction ratio drops.

    Display, not data

    • All collagen the same brightness: it is above the display ceiling (20 nm). Raise the ceiling to see the differences.
    • Mostly one hue in the orientation map: residual retardance left uncorrected (see card 4).
    About the model. Every frame on this page is computed with exact Jones calculus: a linear polarizer, two variable retarders 45° apart, the slide, specimen and optics as retarders, and an ideal circular analyzer. Retardance and angle come from the five-state formulas of Shribak & Oldenbourg (2003), and the background is subtracted as their two coefficients, as OpenPolScope does. Stray light sets an extinction-ratio ceiling of 300. The residual retardances (0.4–3 nm optics, 1–5 nm slides) and the drift rate are illustrative, not measured on our rig.
