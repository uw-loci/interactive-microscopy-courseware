Making an LC-PolScope image, and what each mistake looks like

An LC-PolScope never records a retardance image directly. The camera records five ordinary brightness images, and software turns them into a retardance image and an orientation image. A mistake at any step still produces a clean-looking picture, so you need to know what each mistake does to the result. The concept page explains the physics and the settings page explains each setting. This page follows the data from the microscope to the finished image.

The live panels below use five real frames of a birefringent test target, recorded on our microscope. Your browser runs the same arithmetic as the microscope software, so a change you make here is applied to measured data. On pixels that were not saturated, the results here differ from the images saved by the microscope software by a median of 0.03 nm and 0.11°.

The five frames the software received

All five use one brightness scale, 0 to 65,535 counts. Red marks saturated pixels. State0 is the extinction state, and the other four are the swing states.

The correct panels are partly reconstructed. The frames were recorded at 141.5 ms, long enough to saturate one or more swing states on most spoke pixels. For the correct panels, each saturated value was rebuilt from the unsaturated frame of its pair and the extinction frame, then scaled to 50 ms with simulated camera noise. The 141.5 ms setting of Exposure too long uses the frames exactly as recorded. The arithmetic is otherwise unchanged. The background is a smooth fit to the background the microscope used.

3 · choosing the exposure

How much retardance fits before a swing state saturates

Empty glass is already bright in the swing states, and birefringence makes one of them brighter still. A pixel with high retardance therefore saturates long before empty glass does. Saturation is easy to miss because the saturated pixels are a small part of the frame and the retardance image still looks normal. Move the slider to see the limit for this rig at a swing of 0.05 waves.

A long exposure is not always wrong. Liver sections on this rig read mostly under 5 nm, so 141.5 ms was safe and gave less noise. The test target needs a shorter exposure. Choose the exposure from the brightest structure you intend to measure, then keep it for the whole study. Collect the background at the exposure you will image with.

4 · stitching tiles together

Seams from a missing background, on the same four tiles

Strain in the objective and condenser lenses adds the same pattern to every tile. Each tile looks acceptable alone, but the pattern repeats across a mosaic. Both images below show the same four real tiles of mostly empty glass, displayed at the same scale, with black at 0 nm and white at 4 nm.

The remaining 0.8 nm is real and unexplained so far. It is nearly uniform across the field, and the same size whichever background was used, which suggests it belongs to the glass where these tiles were taken, not to the correction. A uniform offset like this one is too small to matter on a 17 nm spoke, but it would bend the measured angle of a 2 nm fiber.

Bright structures read low, or their angles bunch toward certain directionsSaturated swing stateLook at the raw State frames for the brightest structure. If it reaches the top of the camera range, shorten the exposure, recalibrate, and collect a new background.

About the data. Five frames from one 2048 × 2048 tile, cropped to 260 × 260 pixels, 0.2695 µm per pixel, 20x objective, Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash4.0, 549 nm, swing 0.05 waves, exposure 141.5 ms, extinction ratio 272. Reconstruction follows the five-state formulas of Shribak & Oldenbourg (2003) as implemented by OpenPolScope 3.20b. Simulated camera noise uses 0.46 electrons per count. Orientation colors use the PPM -7° calibration with the angle reference measured on the same target (sign -1, offset 4.5°), so a direction has the same color as on our PPM images.

  • Oldenbourg R, Mei G. New polarized light microscope with precision universal compensator. J. Microsc. 180:140–147 (1995).
